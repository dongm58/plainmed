CS 72/272 Project Proposal: Plain-Language Medical Summaries
1. Project Title and Author
PlainMed: Turning Medical Evidence into Plain Language, with Checks for What the Rewrite Gets Wrong
Michael Dong (individual project)
PlainMed rewrites the technical abstracts of medical systematic reviews into plain English, then flags the parts of its own output a patient should not trust without checking.
2. Problem Statement and Motivation
The most informative summaries of medical literature are written for physicians and not patients. Cochrane systematic reviews summarize the data from many clinical studies and are an important part of any treatment decision process. However, abstracts of these reviews are full of statistical terminology (risk ratio, confidence interval), trial terms (RCT, GRADE, heterogeneity) and long sentences. As shown in our preliminary analysis (Section 5), the average readability grade of abstracts is around college grade level, whereas the health-literacy guideline suggests that material intended for patients be written on much lower levels.
Cochrane requests that the authors prepare an easy-to-understand version of an article manually; however, this process is both time-consuming and subjective. Instead, patients use search engines and chatbots which may be confident in their statements yet distort information intentionally or unintentionally.
Intended users. Patients and caregivers studying diagnosis or a course of treatment; people with low health-literacy or reading English as a second language.
Stakeholders. Practitioners who want to give patients easy-to-understand evidence; health librarians and patient educators; review authors who now manually compose the plain language version.
Social benefit. Improved access to credible health information can contribute to informed decision-making. The danger of the opposite - a fluent but misleading explanation – is the reason why this project makes faithfulness checking a primary focus of the task.
3. Selected NLP Task
Primary Task. Paragraph-level text simplification, which is a type of conditional generation (sequence-to-sequence): input is a results section of a technical abstract, and the output is a simplified paragraph with identical main claims. A secondary classification task is used to help the primary task: classification of sentences from the output as supported or unsupported by the source.
Novelty over previous work. Devaraj et al. (2021) are concerned with simplicity in the generated text, while Guo et al. (2024) are focused on incorporating background information using retrieval techniques. We, however, are interested in whether the text is reliable: we perform the evaluation of generalization to a different, independent source, evaluate the frequency of outputs which contain unsubstantiated claims, and create a checker system to point out to the user which sentences need verification.
Research question. Does fine-tuning or prompting of the model allow for generating more readable Cochrane abstracts without incorporating claims not supported by the source, and would incorporating the faithfulness checker find the remaining errors?
4. Dataset Description
We will use two datasets: a core training and test set of patient-facing Cochrane summaries, and a second, independent test set of UK health research summaries drawn from CELLS. A third dataset, PLABA, is a stretch goal for higher-quality evaluation.

Why can't we train the whole of CELLS? CELLS is 14 times bigger than Cochrane, but our exploration (see Section 5) demonstrates that 91% of it consists of research-science texts (PNAS significance statements and PLOS biology summaries), targeted at educated people, not patients. Moreover, it overlaps with the Cochrane test dataset quite significantly. Therefore, we will use it only in one controlled experiment (see Section 6).
Stretch: PLABA (Attal et al., 2023). 750 PubMed abstracts adapted sentence by sentence into plain language by NIH experts, written specifically for the public. It is hosted on OSF; we will confirm the download before relying on it.
Limitations. (1) References may not be simpler: differences in how diligent the authors are lead to only slightly simplified references for some targets. (2) Targets are not strict translations: targets may provide additional information or lack statistical measures, making our comparison of overlap metrics approximate. (3) Our dataset contains only English biomedical data.

We cloned the Cochrane dataset repository, loaded all three splits, and calculated basic statistics for the training split. Our data is clean: there are no missing fields, no duplicate targets, and no duplicate sources. Our main finding is that the plain-language summaries are more concise and have significantly fewer numbers, but not significantly simpler.

What does this mean? The simplification in our case is mainly the reduction of statistical content and condensation, rather than just replacing difficult words. There is a very small grade level difference (around 1.3 grades), which confirms Limitation 1 above, so it is essential to measure the readability separately from the similarity to the reference. Grade levels were calculated using a heuristic syllable counter; therefore, we will verify it with a readability package.

CELLS: what is in it, and does it overlap with Cochrane?
We unzipped CELLS from GitHub (45,280 train / 11,295 val / 6,311 test pairs) and broke the training split down by journal using its metadata file. Most of it is not patient-facing:
Source journal (CELLS train) | Pairs | Share | Audience
PNAS (significance statements) | 18,352 | 40.5% | Scientists in other fields
PLOS journals (biology, genetics, pathogens, etc.) | 22,865 | 50.5% | Educated general readers
Cochrane | 3,137 | 6.9% | Patients
NIHR health research reports | 926 | 2.0% | Patients and public
In addition, we found 1,769 duplicate pairs in CELLS train. In particular, 227 of the 480 Cochrane test examples are also present in CELLS train dataset (abstract matching). This means that training on CELLS and testing on Cochrane without deduplication would leak test answers into training data and boost performance.
NIHR is a subset of the dataset with inputs longer than those in Cochrane (551-word abstracts, 290-word summaries).
6. Proposed NLP Method and Baseline
We will compare four ways of producing the plain-language paragraph, then run every output through the same faithfulness checker.
Inputs and outputs. Input: one abstract results paragraph (up to about 1,024 subword tokens). Output: a plain-language paragraph, its readability score, and a supported/unsupported flag per sentence.
Preprocessing. Normalize whitespace and Unicode; split sentences with spaCy; tokenize with each model's own subword tokenizer (BPE for BART). We keep numbers in the input because deciding which statistics to keep is part of the task.
Systems compared.
Baseline 1 (no model): return the source unchanged. Any system must beat this on readability.
Baseline 2 (extractive): select the 3 source sentences with the fewest digits and shortest length. This tests whether simply dropping statistic-heavy sentences is enough.
Fine-tuned model: BART-base (Lewis et al., 2020) fine-tuned on the 3,568 training pairs. Devaraj et al. (2021) fine-tuned the larger BART-large-XSum and added an unlikelihood loss that discourages technical vocabulary; I use the smaller model to fit free GPU limits. We will start without that loss and add it if time allows.
Prompted LLM: an instruction-tuned model given the abstract and a fixed prompt (zero-shot, then 3-shot with training examples).
Data experiment: does more general science data make any difference? We will fine-tune BART-base two times: once using only Cochrane, and once using Cochrane as well as CELLS. Prior to the second fine-tuning, we will delete all the CELLS pairs with an abstract matching either validation or test example from Cochrane as well as 1,769 internal duplicates. Comparison of the results of two fine-tunings on both sets will show whether additional 40,000+ research science abstracts have a negative or positive impact on simplification.
Faithfulness checker. Each output sentence gets scored for being entailed by the source text using a pretrained natural language inference (NLI) model in the fashion of SummaC (Laban et al., 2022). Sentences below a threshold become "check this." Mismatches between numbers (percentage in the output is not present in the source) are found using a simple rule.
Tools. Python, PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers and Datasets, spaCy, the evaluate library for metrics, and a free GPU (Dartmouth Discovery cluster or Colab) for fine-tuning BART-base.
7. Interactive Demo Concept
Demo is an application (Gradio/Streamlit running locally), wherein the user pastes in the abstract and gets the simplified version thereof, as well as the problems found with the latter.
Who uses it. The demo tool is used by patients or caregivers/patient educators, who have found a Cochrane review and would like to comprehend it.
What the user does. (1) Paste in the abstract or choose one of the abstracts in the test set. (2) Choose a system (fine-tuned BART, prompted LLM or baseline). (3) Click Simplify.
What the system does. It outputs the simplified paragraph; highlights with a “check this” flag all the sentences marked as problematic by the faithfulness checker; when the user clicks on such a sentence, the source one is shown; and shows the grade of readability before and after the simplification.
Relation to the NLP task. All elements seen on the screen are live outputs of the pipeline described in Section 6; the generator produces text, the NLI checker produces flags, and the readability metric produces a score. Users can even compare two systems with each other on the same abstract, and the evaluation is thereby made visible. Thus, if one model paraphrases “may improve knowledge” into “helps people remember their medicines,” the flagging will be done and connected to the original cautious statement.
8. Evaluation Plan
We will evaluate each system on three dimensions: simplicity, proximity to the expert-generated version, and faithfulness to the original. All results are reported on the held-out 480 examples test split and again on the 1,287 pairs NIHR set to check generalization to a different source. The validation split from Cochrane is used for tuning, e.g., the checker’s threshold.
Question | Metric | Why
Is it simpler? | Flesch-Kincaid grade level (Kincaid et al., 1975); output length; digits per 100 words | Directly measures readability, the goal of the task
Close to the expert summary? | SARI (Xu et al., 2016); ROUGE-L; BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020) | SARI is the standard simplification metric; ROUGE and BERTScore measure content overlap
Faithful to the source? | Share of output sentences flagged by the NLI checker; manual faithfulness labels on a sample | Readability is worthless if the content is wrong
Comparisons to baseline. Each model is benchmarked against the copy of source and extractive baselines (Section 6), while the two neural models are compared to one another.
Checker validation. We will manually label 100 output sentences (sampled evenly from the two neural systems) as either supported or unsupported, and report the checker's precision and recall on those labels. As I am conducting this study alone, I will relabel 30 out of the 100 sentences a week later without seeing my first set of labels, and report self-agreement (Cohen's kappa).
Qualitative analysis. Errors will be classified as follows: (1) hallucinated or altered numbers, (2) increased level of certainty ("helps" when the source says "may help" or "inconclusive"), (3) important findings that have been omitted, (4) jargon terms that were not explained.
9. Ethical, Privacy, Fairness, and Safety Considerations
Safety. A wrong simplification could change someone's health decision. The app will state that it is a reading aid, not medical advice, always show the original next to the rewrite, and flag doubtful sentences rather than hide them.
Privacy. The dataset contains only published review text, with no patient records. The demo will not log what users paste. If we use a hosted LLM API, we will say so on screen so users do not paste personal health details.
Fairness. Readability formulas are tuned to English and to average readers; a grade-8 score does not guarantee a text is clear to every group. We will name this limitation and not claim accessibility for languages or populations we did not test.
Over-trust. A polished interface can make outputs feel authoritative. The "check this" flags and visible source are meant to keep users skeptical in the right places.
Attribution. We will credit Cochrane and Devaraj et al. as the data source, and document every pretrained model, API, and AI tool used, per course policy.
